This study presented PRISM and AUDA as a reproducible workflow for turning heterogeneous plastic-waste source records into analytical datasets and for benchmarking reconstruction and forecasting methods on those datasets. The experiments covered correlation analysis, feature importance, missing-value reconstruction, univariate temporal forecasting, and multivariate forecasting across country-level and global plastic-waste indicators. Together, these experiments show that sparse environmental datasets require careful modeling and evaluation: the best-performing method changes with the target variable, country, sample size, and trend shape.

The empirical results support a practical conclusion. Flexible machine learning models can be effective when enough signal is available, as shown by the strong performance of SVR on the global plastics production forecasting task and by kernel-based models in several reconstruction and multivariate forecasting settings. However, simpler statistical and baseline methods remain highly competitive, and in short country-level series they can outperform more flexible alternatives. This is especially important for plastic-waste data, where many countries and indicators have fewer than a few dozen observations and where reporting practices vary across sources.

The proposed selection-efficiency coefficient for the one-standard-error rule provides a useful way to control the trade-off between validation performance and model simplicity. In the reconstruction sensitivity study, \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.1\) often changed the selected configuration while introducing only a small validation-performance penalty. This suggests that modest standard-error tolerance can reduce hyperparameter-selection instability without materially degrading predictive accuracy, although the appropriate tolerance should still be checked for each modeling setting.

Several limitations remain. The AUDA analytical tables are built from verified records, but the underlying source data are still sparse, noisy, and heterogeneous. Some evaluation splits contain very few test observations, making metrics such as \(R^2\) unstable and making model rankings sensitive to small changes in the data. The current driver-based forecasting tasks also do not explicitly include policy interventions, infrastructure investment, or regulatory variables that may strongly affect future plastic-waste generation. Future work should expand the set of validated country-year records, incorporate policy and waste-management covariates, quantify uncertainty more explicitly, and test whether pooled or transfer-learning approaches can improve country-specific forecasts without masking local behavior.

Overall, AUDA provides a foundation for reproducible plastic-waste modeling under realistic data constraints. The results indicate that robust forecasting in this domain depends less on choosing a universally superior model and more on matching model complexity, validation strategy, and interpretation to the amount and quality of data available.
