In Experiment 4, we evaluate the sensitivity of the hyperparameter-selection procedure to the standard-error tolerance coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{SE}}\) and the complexity ordering in Experiment 3 (United States). We perform a sweep over \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} \in \{0, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1\}\) and compare the selected models with the minimum mean validation-error configurations in terms of validation WAPE. At \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0\), candidates are determined by exact floating-point comparisons with the minimum mean validation error; no numerical tie tolerance is applied. If more than one candidate remains, the implementation selects the lexicographically smallest complexity tuple in the order specified in Table~\ref{tab:complexity-ordering}. When \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 1\), the threshold corresponds to the standard 1-SE rule. We record the number of candidate configurations that fall within the selection threshold, the percentage of selection units where the selected configuration changes, the minimum validation WAPE, and the validation WAPE of the final selected configuration. The penalty is their difference. The complexity rank of a configuration is its position when all evaluated configurations are ordered from simplest to most complex. We record the complexity rank of the selected configuration.

We also evaluate the sensitivity to the complexity ordering by comparing the selected configurations when the signs of the complexity-ordering keys are flipped.

In Experiment 3, we repeatedly perform 16 imputation runs and record the mean of results. In this experiment, we evaluate the sensitivity of the hyperparameter selection on three tunable models: ridge regression, GPR, and SVR. Therefore, there are 48 selection units for this experiment.

The sweep results in Figure~\ref{fig:selection_sensitivity_to_se_tolerance} and Table~\ref{tab:selection_sensitivity} show that \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.1\) changes model selection in more than half of the selection units for each model while adding a small WAPE penalty (\(0.01\%\) for ridge regression and GPR, and \(0.06\%\) for SVR). When \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.01\), the WAPE penalty for all three models is 0.00\% at the precision reported in the table. Larger coefficients continue to select simpler configurations, but \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.5\) and \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 1\) lead to larger validation-performance penalties, particularly for SVR, where the WAPE penalty increases to 0.43\% and 1.10\%, respectively. At \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0\), selection is determined by the minimum mean validation error, with the complexity ordering used only to resolve ties. When \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.01\), the number of candidate configurations for the three models ranges from \(6.44\) to \(10.69\).

At \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.01\), the selected configuration differs from the minimum mean validation-error configuration in a substantial fraction of selection units while producing a 0.00\% average WAPE penalty at the precision reported in Table~\ref{tab:selection_sensitivity}. In this benchmark, \(c_{\mathrm{SE}} = 0.01\) therefore provides an empirical trade-off between validation error and model simplicity. We use this value in the subsequent experiments.

The results in Table~\ref{tab:complexity_ordering_robustness} illustrate that flipping the signs of the complexity-ordering keys changed the selected configuration in 81.25--93.75\% of selection units. This confirms that the complexity ordering actively influences the selection. Nevertheless, the mean WAPE degradation is negligible (\(<0.01\%\)), indicating that configurations within the selection threshold have nearly identical validation WAPE in this benchmark.

The complexity-ordering sensitivity analysis shows that changing the ordering can alter the selected configuration even when the associated validation WAPE changes very little. Thus, the complexity ordering is an explicit design choice that determines which configuration is selected from the candidate set defined by \(c_{\mathrm{SE}}\). While this benchmark evaluates that trade-off, it does not demonstrate that the rule prevents hyperparameter overfitting.
